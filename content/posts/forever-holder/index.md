Imagine someone who stops making great products because "I have enough money." Now imagine someone who starts a company only for the money.

Both have misunderstood what drives the work. **Innovation is a character trait. Money doesn't control it.** The people who build things that last keep going long after money stops being a reason, because the problem won't leave them alone and because they can see their work helping others.

That's where this essay starts. It ends with Bitcoin, and with an argument for why its price, measured over a long enough time, has no ceiling. To get there, we first need to be clear about value, price, and time.

## Value is created for others, and by others

"I love this" on its own is a hobby. "I love this, and others love it too" is something bigger. Once other people get real value from what you make, the work stops being only yours. Their use and appreciation become a reason to keep going that doesn't depend on your mood on a given day.

Value is also never created alone. Behind every product there is a chain of people who helped: suppliers, toolmakers, teachers, farmers who fed the workers, and even the security forces who protect the land the minerals came from. Each of them spent hours of their life so that you could build what you built.

So I'd put it this way:

> **Price comes from value created for others and by others.**

The buyer pays for the value they receive. The money then flows back down the chain to everyone who made that value possible. Charging for your work isn't greed. It's how you thank thousands of people you will never meet.

## Doing the same with less

Scale and efficiency matter because they lower the price. That doesn't make value smaller. It either reaches more people, or it reaches the same people using less help, which frees that help for somewhere else.

Farming is the classic example. Most people once had to farm so everyone could eat. Today a small fraction produces more food than ever, and everyone else is free to heal, build, teach and invent. Nothing was lost. The help moved to where it was needed next.

## What gives something a price

Scarcity alone doesn't give something a price. My handwritten shopping list is one of a kind, and nobody would pay for it. A thing has a price only when it is **both scarce and valuable to someone else**.

Each buyer quietly compares two versions of their life: one with the product and one without. If the difference is worth the price, they buy. That comparison is personal. A car is worth a lot to someone living far from town and little to someone next to a metro station. A starving person won't give a sandwich for a Lamborghini, and if they accept one, they'll sell it as fast as they can to buy food.

Perception matters too. People pay enormous sums for watches, brands and status, and there's no objective line saying which of those values are "real." We also never know whether a buyer sees the full picture. Probably nobody does. What we can control is our own side: whether we honestly offer what we believe helps.

### Price discovery needs only one thing: asking

You don't need to know the right price in advance. Asking for money is enough to find it:

1. The seller names a price.
2. The buyer accepts or offers less.
3. The seller lowers the price or waits for another buyer.
4. This repeats until a real buyer and a real seller meet.

The buyer has a ceiling, set by their with-or-without comparison. The seller has a floor, set by the chain of time and help that went into the product. Waiting costs both sides time, and that pressure pushes them toward each other.

### Don't discount without a reason

A seller shouldn't cut prices because "I have enough money." A discount is fine when it does a job: scale lowered your costs, you're rewarding early supporters, or you want to reach people who would otherwise be left out. A discount for its own sake buys nothing. It's like paying for a subscription you never use. **By default, you charge the right price.** If you want to give, give separately and deliberately.

## Ultimately, what's scarce is time

Follow almost any price down the chain and you end up at people's time: hours spent designing, mining, transporting, teaching, protecting. Everyone gets the same 24 hours a day, and none of them come back.

When you earn more than you consume, the surplus is **stored time**: work you did for others that you haven't claimed back yet. Then the question is where to store it, so that nobody can print more of the thing you saved in and quietly spend part of your time.

That's where Bitcoin comes in. Its supply is fixed at 21 million coins, and no one can create more.

## The forever holder

Now for the core of the argument.

Imagine just ten people who each buy $1 of Bitcoin every day, regardless of price, and never sell. That sounds like nothing. But pick any price you like, say $1 trillion per coin, and ask whether the price could stay below it forever.

If it did, those ten people would collect at least 10 ÷ 1 trillion coins every day. That's tiny, but it never stops, and after enough days it adds up to more than 21 million coins. That can't happen, because there aren't that many coins. So the price can't stay below $1 trillion forever. The same reasoning works for any number you can name.

> **A buyer who never sells, however small, guarantees that the price will eventually pass any number you can imagine.**

"Eventually" matters. The logic says nothing about speed. With ten people at $1 a day, it could take longer than the age of the universe. The size of the flow decides whether it takes years or eons. The structure of the argument stays the same.

### No one has to live forever

The obvious objection is that no person or company holds forever. Companies sell. Governments change their minds. Even the largest corporate holder, famous for its "never sell" stance, [sold bitcoin in 2026](https://www.theblock.co/news/markets/2026-08-03-michael-saylor-says-never-sold-his-personal-bitcoin-strategy-dumps-more-btc-410451) to fund dividends.

But the argument doesn't need any single holder to be immortal. Look at holders by cohort. Of the people who buy this year, some will hold for two years, some for five, some for ten. If **even a small, stable fraction of each year's buyers never sells**, maybe 0.5% or 1%, then that fraction exists again next year, and the year after, and so on.

Most cohorts only delay supply, because their coins eventually return to the market. The forever fraction is a one-way door: coins go in and never come out. With a new cohort every year, the door is used every year. The same proof applies. The only requirement is that the total money flowing into that forever fraction, added up over all future years, grows without limit. With steady saving, plus inflation, it does.

This isn't only theory. Because every transaction is public, you can see how long coins sit still, and a [large share of all bitcoin hasn't moved in years](https://coincentral.com/bitcoins-lost-forever-statistics/), some of it for more than a decade. Long-holding cohorts clearly exist, and they aren't small.

### Why "forever" is natural, not a promise

This is the part I find most convincing. The forever fraction doesn't require anyone to promise anything.

**Nobody knows how long they will live, or how much they will need.** So a careful saver never plans to spend down to zero. They keep a reserve "just in case," which means planning as if they'll live forever. As a result, almost everyone dies with something left. Economists call these *accidental bequests*: money left behind not by choice, but because uncertainty made a buffer rational until the very end.

When that saver dies, their coins are either lost (no one has the keys), inherited by someone with the same habit, or inherited and sold. As long as the first two outweigh the third, each generation adds to the forever fraction without anyone intending to.

That's what makes it an entity that doesn't depend on anyone's decision. Nobody decides to hold forever. People simply never reach the point where they're sure they can let go.

## More value, bigger reserves

This is where the beginning and the end meet.

The forever reserve isn't a fixed amount. **It grows with the value people create.** Creating more value for others brings higher income. Higher income doesn't have to mean more consumption, so the surplus grows, and with it the reserve that never gets spent. Efficiency spreads this across the whole economy, because doing the same with less leaves more people with a surplus to protect.

And it can't be otherwise. The reserve is where the reward for creating value is kept. If the reserve had a cap, value created beyond that point would have nowhere to be stored. It would leak away, get diluted, or never be worth creating. **Putting a cap on the reserve is putting a limit on value creation.** A store of value that has to hold whatever humanity creates can't have a ceiling on what it's worth.

The full chain:

1. People create value for others, with others' help.
2. They're paid, often more than they need to consume.
3. Uncertainty about the future makes them keep part of the surplus as a permanent reserve.
4. The more value is created, the bigger that reserve becomes. It has to be that way: putting a cap on the reserve would put a limit on value creation.
5. If the reserve is held in something with a fixed supply, a growing flow is chasing a fixed number of coins. The price per coin has no upper limit in the long run.

Seen this way, Bitcoin's long-term price isn't speculation at its core. It reflects how much value humanity keeps creating and keeps saving.

We can already see pieces of this. El Salvador has [bought one bitcoin every day since November 2022](https://cryptobriefing.com/el-salvador-buys-bitcoin-daily-strategy/). The number of public companies holding bitcoin [reached at least 154 by April 2026](https://www.kucoin.com/blog/public-companies-holding-bitcoin), with newcomers still arriving. Each of them is temporary. The flow they form together is what matters.

## Even these don't push it back

There are real objections to this argument. None of them reverses its direction. They change how fast it moves and how bumpy the path is, not where it leads.

- **"Forever" can't be observed in advance.** True: a coin that hasn't moved in ten years looks the same as one that will move tomorrow. But the argument doesn't need to know which coins are forever-held. It only needs some to be, and lost coins plus the reserves people keep until death make that close to certain.
- **Bitcoin doesn't have to win all the savings.** Stocks, property and gold compete for the same pool. But the argument never needed Bitcoin to win. A small, steady share of a growing pool is enough, because any flow that never stops adds up without limit.
- **Speed is unknown.** "Eventually" may mean decades or much longer. Speed changes when the price passes a given number, not whether it does.
- **Booms and busts come in waves.** Buyers crowd in during good times and sellers during bad times, so the price moves in pulses with deep drawdowns. But busts release coins from short-term cohorts. The forever share absorbed in each boom stays absorbed, so every cycle starts from a smaller float.
- **Heirs can sell.** Some will. The door only leaks back fully if nearly every heir sells and no keys are ever lost. As long as some inherited coins are kept or lost, the door stays one-way overall.

Each objection is a reason the climb won't be smooth or fast. None is a reason it turns around.

## Conclusion

Innovation is character, and character creates value. Value is created for others and by others, and price is how that value moves back through the chain. The surplus people earn is stored time, and because nobody knows how long they will live, part of it is never spent.

If even a small part of that never-spent reserve keeps flowing, generation after generation, into an asset whose supply can't grow, then that asset's price will eventually pass any number you can imagine. That's not because people intend it, but because people keep creating value, keep saving for a future they can't see, and keep leaving something behind.
