The question sounds simple: do we need a government for the entire world? Pandemics, pollution, shared oceans and the risks of new technology all cross borders. No single country can solve them alone. So the case for a global authority seems obvious.

But the closer I look at it, the less a world government resembles anything we already know.

## A government with no outside

A country is defined partly by what lies beyond it. A border only means something because there is another country on the other side. Armies, border controls and economic strategy all exist *in relation to* other states: something to protect against, someone to compete with.

A global government would have no outside. There would be no alternative to it, no other country to compare it with, and nowhere to go if it failed you. It would not be a bigger version of a national government. It would be a different kind of thing altogether.

And yes, there would still be local governments, just as countries have regions and cities. But the umbrella on top would face a problem national governments don't.

## What countries can't do alone

If global governance makes sense at all, it is for things individual countries cannot achieve. Countries are defined by their land, their people and their resources. What falls outside that division?

- **What no one owns:** the atmosphere, the high seas, the deep seabed, Antarctica, outer space.
- **What crosses borders:** pollution, disease, migration, money, data, and above all narratives and media.
- **What spills over:** a country's own land and resources can affect everyone else.

Most of these come down to one pattern: **one country gains and another pays.** An upstream dam produces power while downstream countries lose water. One country's fleets overfish shared waters, and the fish disappear for everyone. Fires clearing land in one country fill a neighbor's skies with smoke. And a narrative pushed through one country's media can shape how millions of people elsewhere see their neighbors, their history and themselves. Whoever tells the story gains influence; those who are described by it pay the price.

Inside a country, courts and laws settle these disputes. Between countries, there is negotiation, and the stronger side usually wins.

## What if each country is simply responsible?

One alternative is to let each country be fully responsible for its own territory and any harm that spreads from it. International law already partly works this way, and agreements like the Paris climate accord rely on countries setting their own commitments.

It works when harm is direct and traceable. It breaks down on three questions: who judges when countries disagree, who enforces when the guilty party is powerful, and who is responsible when harm comes from everyone at once?

## Every enforcer belongs to someone

So perhaps we need an enforcer. But in what form? A confederation? A police force? A leading country?

The world is already divided between countries. Any organization we create must be funded, staffed and protected by them, and so it ends up under the influence of whoever supplies the money and the force. Today's global institutions show this clearly: permanent vetoes, votes weighted by financial contribution.

Rotating power sounds fairer. But then: who goes first? Who decides the order? Whoever decided the order, who gave *them* the authority?

## The chain that leads back to nothing

This is the deepest problem. Every decision gets its authority from an earlier decision. Trace the chain back and you eventually reach a first moment that nothing authorized. In calm times nobody asks. In a conflict, everyone does, and the whole structure becomes open to challenge.

Philosophers call this the paradox of founding. A flawless origin is impossible, for two reasons. First, the first authority cannot be authorized by anything before it, or it wouldn't be first. It can only authorize itself, and that is circular. Second, even a perfect beginning would not bind those who come later. My great-grandparents' consent is not my consent.

So legitimacy cannot be inherited from the past. It has to be renewed in the present, by people accepting it again and again. Countries survive this because long habits of shared identity keep that acceptance going. A global body would start with none, and its first serious conflict would show it. The question "who decides?" has no answer at the top. It can only be answered from the bottom.

## Why should I vote for someone who doesn't stand in my shoes?

Voting doesn't solve it either. Why should I give my vote to a country, or a candidate, who doesn't stand on my land and doesn't live my life? A small nation would be permanently outvoted by the largest ones on matters concerning its own ground.

Imagine the whole world electing one person. Eight billion voters, hundreds of languages, and a winner who inevitably carries the view of one corner of the planet. It is a joke. The idea falls apart the moment you picture it.

And it certainly cannot come into existence because a few powerful people decide it should. Every past attempt to unite the world from the top, by emperors and empires, was eventually seen as domination and broken apart.

## The world as home

So the real question is not how to design a world government. It is how people come to see the world as **home**.

At home, a few people live together, and nobody needs a government on top of them. Everything is shared. There is no "other" inside the walls, and no single person stands above everyone else.

This planet is our home. This world is our home. The country is our home. These are not competing loyalties. They are layers, and one can hold all of them at once.

That is also why no country is truly safe on its own. Living in a world where one country goes to hell doesn't feel secure, even if its troubles seem far away. It is like a room lit by many lamps: take one light away, and the entire room loses light.

An identity like this can grow from the bottom up. It can start with a single person, then a family, a city, a country, and eventually the world. It lasts when it is resilient and attractive enough that people *choose* it. The moment it is imposed, it becomes an empire and invites resistance.

In the home I imagine, there is no chaos. Things simply work, and everyone feels trusted.

No home means chaos. But chaos works against itself. When things fall apart, those who see the world as home become desired. People look for them, and the chaos itself pushes them up.

Those who seek benefit from chaos gain too, for a while. They get a free relation with the rules: they can pick them up, bend them or drop them as it suits them. But the same chaos that lifts them eventually brings them down. Once people have lived through what they offer, they belong to the past.

So in the end, chaos serves the idea of home. It shows everyone what has been lost, and it raises up those who can rebuild it.

## Fixing yourself is fixing the world, and fixing the world is fixing yourself

There is no real distinction between fixing yourself, fixing your family, fixing your workplace, your city, your country and the world. The same action serves all of them at once. Being honest and fair at home is not a step *before* changing the world. It is already part of it. And it works both ways: whatever I do for the world changes me too.

For me, that means no longer following those who don't see the problem this way. Leaders only have power because people follow them, and today much of that following happens through media: what we watch, share and repeat. Every narrative of "us versus them" lives on attention. But not following someone is different from shutting them out. They live in the same home.

The line I draw is this: **I don't follow them, but I don't stop talking to them.**

Or, in one word: **talk.** And it isn't easy to know where to draw that line, which is exactly why talking matters. I find out whom to trust by talking, not by deciding in advance.

They may not talk with me. They may block me out, especially when they are betting on my absence. A story of "us versus them" survives only as long as the other side is not in the room. That is no reason for me to stop talking. It is a reason to stay present, because presence is the one thing they cannot afford.

And talking does not mean saying what they would like to hear, or holding back what they dislike. I just say my words.
