"Competition makes things better." Are there cases where it doesn't?

Plenty. The claim holds when what wins the competition is the same thing you actually want. It breaks when those two come apart, and that happens in a few recognizable ways.

## When winning means making someone else bear the cost

If firms can cut costs by polluting, skimping on safety, or squeezing workers, competition rewards whoever does that most. This is the "race to the bottom," and it's why markets need rules around them. Competition among countries for investment through lower taxes or looser regulation can work the same way.

## When the prize is relative, not absolute

Some contests only rank people. Every applicant gets more tutoring, more credentials, more unpaid internships, and the number of spots at the top stays fixed. Everyone pays more and nobody ends up better off. Arms races, luxury status goods, and the peacock's tail (costly for every peacock, useful only relative to the others) all follow this pattern.

## When the measure isn't the goal

Competition optimizes whatever gets scored. Social media platforms compete for engagement, and outrage engages well. Schools that compete on test scores teach to the test. Researchers who compete on publication counts produce more papers but not always more truth. This is Goodhart's law in competitive form.

## When buyers can't judge quality

Kenneth Arrow argued that healthcare doesn't work like a normal market, because patients can't easily tell good treatment from bad. Where buyers can't judge quality, sellers may compete on marketing, reassurance, or price instead, and quality can actually fall. The used-car "market for lemons" is the classic example.

## When one provider is simply more efficient

Water pipes, power grids, and railways cost so much to build that running two parallel networks wastes resources. These are usually regulated monopolies for that reason. Standards are similar. A single shared electrical plug or internet protocol beats ten rival ones.

## When cooperation is the thing producing value

Science advances by sharing results, and too much competition encourages hoarding data and rushing sloppy work. Within teams and families, setting people against each other often destroys the trust that makes the group work.

## When it crowds out intrinsic motivation

Turning an activity people enjoy, like creative work, learning, or play, into a ranked contest can make the people who aren't winning lose interest in it.

## The short version

- **Races to the bottom:** firms or countries win by pushing costs onto others, through pollution, unsafe products, low wages, or tax cuts that drain public budgets.
- **Zero-sum status contests:** when only rank matters, everyone spends more (credentials, tutoring, arms, luxury goods) and nobody ends up better off.
- **Gaming the metric (Goodhart's law):** competition optimizes whatever gets scored. Platforms chase engagement and get outrage, and schools teach to the test.
- **Buyers can't judge quality:** in healthcare, used cars, or financial advice, sellers can win on marketing or price instead, and quality falls.
- **Natural monopolies:** for water, power grids, and railways, building duplicate networks wastes money, so one regulated provider works better.
- **Standards and network effects:** one shared plug, protocol, or format beats many rival ones.
- **Work that depends on cooperation:** in science, teams, and families, competition can lead to hoarding information, rushed work, and lost trust.
- **Crowding out intrinsic motivation:** turning learning, creativity, or play into a ranking can make people lose interest.

## Conclusion

A fairer version of the slogan might be: "Competition makes things better at whatever it rewards, so make sure it rewards the right thing." Much of good policy and good management comes down to designing the scoring so that winning and being good point in the same direction.

In short: competition improves whatever it rewards. It only makes things better when winning and being good are the same thing.
