"Competition makes everything better."

People say this so often that it sounds like a law of nature. It isn't. Competition makes things better at *whatever it rewards*. When what it rewards is the same as what we actually want, it works well. When the two come apart, competition can make things worse, and sometimes it costs lives.

This essay starts with the ordinary exceptions to the slogan and then follows one of them, narrative, to where it leads: power, monopoly, truth, and the one principle that might keep a powerful narrative honest.

## Where competition fails

I covered the general cases in [When Competition Doesn't Make Things Better](/posts/when-competition-fails/): when winning means making someone else bear the cost, when the prize is relative rather than absolute, when the measure isn't the goal, when buyers can't judge quality, when one provider is simply more efficient, when cooperation is what produces value, and when competition crowds out intrinsic motivation.

One case from that list is the subject here: **when the prize is telling the story.**

## Winners write the story

Narratives compete. In news, politics, and history, stories fight for attention and belief. The winner of any contest, whether a war, a market, or an election, usually gets to tell the story of how it won. The winner owns the media, funds the institutions, and writes the textbooks.

That story then makes the win look legitimate ("we won because we were better"), legitimacy protects the winner's position, and the protected position pays for more storytelling. The loop feeds itself, and the narrative drifts toward monopoly.

The usual answer is to write a counter-narrative, one that serves the people the winner's story leaves out. That is ordinary competition, and it's fine. Story against story is a real contest. People can compare the two and choose.

The deeper problem lies somewhere else.

## The narrative against the void

Sometimes a narrative isn't competing with another narrative. It's competing with *nothing*: with silence, an empty space, the absence of any story.

In that contest, whatever exists wins automatically. Its quality doesn't matter. The only bar is being there. Existence beats non-existence, and existence isn't necessarily the better thing.

So a narrative made just to have a narrative is a race to the bottom. When the motive is filling the space rather than telling something true or worth telling, competition rewards speed, volume, and emotional pull. This happens:

- after a crisis, when the first explanation, often a rumor, spreads before any facts are known;
- when "we need our own story" becomes the reason to produce one, even for a good cause;
- when an uncomfortable silence gets filled with anything at all, because people dislike not knowing.

The discipline this calls for is uncomfortable. Instead of always making sure some story exists, we have to be able to **tolerate having no story until we have one worth telling**. That can mean saying "we don't know yet" and holding back from the first explanation that offers relief.

### When it costs lives

This is not an academic point. In politics between states, both mistakes have killed people.

Before the 2003 invasion of Iraq, the claim that Iraq held weapons of mass destruction became the dominant narrative, built on weak and selectively used intelligence. No such weapons were found. The war and what followed killed hundreds of thousands of people. A story rushed in to fill the uncertainty was part of what went wrong.

In Rwanda in 1994, the opposite happened. Foreign governments hesitated and avoided even the word "genocide," partly because naming it would have created pressure to act. In about a hundred days, roughly 800,000 people were killed. There, "we don't know enough yet" served as an excuse.

So "tolerate no story" can't be an absolute rule. It is a discipline against stories that exist only to fill a space. It becomes complicity when silence is chosen because it is convenient. In both cases the failure is the same: *interest* chose the story, or the absence of one, instead of truth.

## Truth, power, and resistance

That raises an objection: isn't truth itself a narrative?

In one sense, yes. Every telling of the truth involves choices: which facts come first, which words describe them, what gets left out. Even choosing a single word is a voice and an act. There is no view from nowhere.

A map makes the point. Every map selects, and none shows everything. Still, maps aren't all equal, because a map that leaves out a cliff can get you killed. The territory is still there, and it decides which maps fail. Truth, once spoken, takes the form of a narrative, but a truthful narrative answers to something outside itself.

The usual worry is that if truth is only narrative, the strongest storyteller decides what's true and the winners' monopoly becomes complete. That worry assumes monopoly and power are the enemy. They don't have to be.

**You cannot resist malicious narratives without power, and effective power tends toward monopoly.** Spreading power equally to everyone is not automatically safer. Nobody would feel safe if every person had a key to a nuclear weapon. We accept that some powers, like armies, courts, and the nuclear launch, belong in very few hands.

"Resist" matters more than "fight" here. Fighting means attacking and conquering, which is how a protective power turns aggressive. Resisting means holding ground and refusing to give way. Malicious narratives often don't win by beating anyone. **They win through our absence.** Resistance starts with being present.

Even the nuclear monopoly isn't handed to one person without limits. It comes with a two-person rule, a chain of command, and procedures designed so that no single individual can act alone. It is a monopoly, but a constrained one.

So the danger is not monopoly or power as such. **The danger is a monopoly with nothing inside it that can say no.**

## The single "no"

That sentence needs more precision, because a "no" can come from anywhere: fear, self-interest, or simply wanting to buy something one couldn't otherwise afford. A veto without a ground is just another kind of power.

The inner "no" should therefore rest on **one principle, and only one**:

> No narrative against the void.

No story may exist merely to fill the empty space, merely for its own existence. Nothing else is added. Every extra principle adds content, and content can eventually turn against the principle itself.

This single principle has three properties.

**It is minimal.** It doesn't say which story is right, only that a story must have a reason beyond occupying space. So it can't become a monopoly on content.

**It survives being applied to itself.** Many anti-authority claims fail this test. "All narratives are lies" is itself a narrative, so it defeats itself. "No narrative against the void" passes. The principle doesn't exist to fill a void. It exists to prevent races to the bottom, so it doesn't contradict its own existence.

**It belongs to no one, and therefore to everyone.** No one can own it, not even whoever first states it, so anyone can take it up. Language works the same way. Nobody owns it, everybody speaks it, and it stays alive only because people keep using it. "Belongs to no one" guards against monopoly, and "belongs to everyone" guards against abandonment.

### Who says no

Someone still has to judge whether a narrative exists merely against the void. That judge can't be a committee or an office, because offices get captured. It can only be a single person who *feels* it. You feel the lack of security when a narrative comes against you, even in a single breath, and nobody has to explain it to you.

That feeling alone isn't enough, though. Feelings can be manufactured, and we feel threats to ourselves more easily than threats to others. So the person holding the "no" has one simple purpose: **no narrative against the void**. Insecurity is how they notice. The purpose decides what they act on.

The single purpose closes both gaps. A narrative that manufactures false fear just to occupy space is itself a narrative against the void, which is exactly what the guardian exists to catch. And the purpose is impersonal. The question is never "is this against me?" but "does this exist only to fill the void?", and it applies to everyone's stories alike.

A rule with one clause is hard to bend, because there is no room for a second agenda to creep in.

## Conclusion

Competition between narratives is fine when the narratives are actually competing, story against story, each trying to be truer or better. Competition becomes a race to the bottom when narratives compete only against the void, because then existence wins and quality loses.

Power, even monopoly, may be necessary to resist malicious narratives that win through our absence. Such power is defensible only with one thing inside it that can refuse, held by someone whose only purpose is this:

**No narrative against the void.**
